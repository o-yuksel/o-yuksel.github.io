Why selection on tit-for-tat flips with population size

In a classic model of cooperation (Nowak et al. 2004, Fig 1A), natural selection favors a new tit-for-tat player only in populations of middle size, so selection flips twice as the population grows. Each flip has its own cause, which you can switch off below. Splitting the population into groups can remove Flip 2 (small groups) or add a third flip (large groups):

  • Very small populations (2 or 3 players). A lone tit-for-tat never meets its own kind, only the few rivals it competes with, and its friendly first move helps each of them (Flip 1).
  • Large populations (over about 1,700). Tit-for-tat only pays once it is common, and in a large population luck rarely carries one copy that far (Flip 2).
  • Populations split into large groups. Once tit-for-tat wins one group, it almost always wins the others, so adding groups does not lower its chance, while a neutral mutation’s chance keeps falling (Flip 3).
  • The game. Two players meet and play 10 rounds. In each round, each player cooperates or cheats.
  • Points per round. Both cooperate: 3 each. One cheats: the cheat gets 5 (the temptation, T, which a slider below changes), the cooperator 0. Both cheat: 1 each.
  • The two strategies. Tit-for-tat cooperates first, then copies its partner’s last move. Always-defect cheats every round. So when they meet, tit-for-tat is cheated in round 1, and both cheat from then on.
  • Totals over 10 rounds. Two tit-for-tats: 30 each. Two defectors: 10 each. Tit-for-tat against a defector: 9, the defector 14.
  • Evolution. Players with more points leave more offspring.
  • “Favored” means that one new tit-for-tat takes over the population more often than a neutral mutation would. Among N players, a neutral mutation takes over with a chance of 1 in N, so the chance tit-for-tat must beat gets smaller as the population grows.

1Flip 1: your partner is your rival

It never meets its own kind. A lone tit-for-tat plays only defectors, since nobody plays against itself, so all the help from its first-round cooperation goes to its rivals: each gets 4 more points than it would from another defector (14 instead of 10). In a population of N, that raises each rival’s average by 4/(N−1): a lot when N is small, almost nothing when N is large.

2Flip 2: too rare to get going

It only pays once it is common. Against a defector, tit-for-tat earns 9 points and the defector 14; two defectors earn 10 each, and two tit-for-tats 30 each. Tit-for-tat beats defectors only once it makes up about 1 in 17 of the population. One new copy has to reach that share by luck, and as the population grows, its chance of doing so falls faster than a neutral mutation’s chance of taking over.

3Flip 3: from one group to the rest

Take groups of 2,000 at T = 5. With one group, a new tit-for-tat is 0.7 times as likely as a neutral mutation to take over, held back as in Flip 2. With two groups it is 1.4 times as likely. Once it wins one group it almost always wins the other, so its own chance stays the same while the neutral chance halves. Its migrants usually fail in a defector group, as in Flip 2, but now and then one succeeds. A defector that migrates into a tit-for-tat group earns 14 points a game to its neighbours’ 30 and almost never takes over, so groups switch almost only one way.

Where the numbers come from: method and sources

Method

As in the w = 0.1 curve of Nowak et al.'s Fig 1A, a single tit-for-tat (TFT) player starts among N−1 always-defect (ALLD) players in a Moran process. They play a 10-round repeated Prisoner's Dilemma with T, R, P, S = 5, 3, 1, 0, giving payoffs a = 30 (TFT against TFT), b = 9 (TFT against ALLD), c = 14 (ALLD against TFT) and d = 10 (ALLD against ALLD). With i tit-for-tat players, fitness is fi = 1 − w + w[a(i−1) + b(N−i)]/(N−1) for tit-for-tat and gi = 1 − w + w[c i + d(N−i−1)]/(N−1) for always-defect. The fixation probability is ρ = 1/(1 + Σk=1N−1 Πi=1k gi/fi), computed exactly in logarithms. The chart plots N·ρ: a neutral mutation takes over with probability 1/N, so above 1 tit-for-tat is favored.

Switch 1 (players can meet themselves) replaces these with fi = 1 − w + w[a i + b(N−i)]/N and gi = 1 − w + w[c i + d(N−i)]/N. Switch 2 (being cheated in round 1 costs nothing) sets b = d = 10 and leaves c unchanged. T sets c = T + 9. Nowak's value is T = 5. Between 3 and 6 the game is a standard Prisoner's Dilemma (T > R and 2R > T + S). Above 6, taking turns at cheating would pay more than steady cooperation, so the game is no longer standard; tit-for-tat and always-defect never take turns, so the model's numbers stay valid.

Groups, rare-migration limit: with D groups of n (total N = nD), N·ρ = N·ρA(n)·(1 − r)/(1 − rD), where ρA(n) and ρB(n) are the exact within-group fixation probabilities of one tit-for-tat among defectors and of one defector among tit-for-tat, and r = ρB(n)/ρA(n). Migration is taken to be rare compared with takeover within a group, and every group sends out migrants at the same rate. Each group then holds one strategy whenever a migrant arrives, so the number of tit-for-tat groups moves up or down one at a time, each step down being r times as likely as a step up. Starting from one tit-for-tat group, this gives the chance (1 − r)/(1 − rD) of reaching all D groups. Populations no larger than n form a single group, so there the curve is Nowak's.

Crossings are found on a log grid of about 200 sizes from 2 to 10,000 and refined by integer bisection; the page reports the first or last favored N; with groups on, the curve treats the number of groups N/n as continuous, so it passes through every whole-group value (N = n, 2n, 3n, …) and is smooth between them. With both switches off, the curve crosses 1 between N = 3 and 4 and between N = 1,732 and 1,733. The first matches the weak-selection minimum of 3.07 that follows from the formula in Nowak et al.'s Fig 1 caption; the paper shows the second only in its figure.

Details

Flip 1, small populations: your partner is your rival

In Nowak et al.'s model a player's payoff is its average over games with all the others, never with itself. A lone tit-for-tat therefore plays only defectors, and each of those games gives the defector T−1 more points than a game between two defectors (4 at Nowak’s T = 5). At N = 2, its only partner is its only rival. In a population of N, each defector's average payoff rises by (T−1)/(N−1), so the effect fades as N grows. Measured against the whole population, a player's relatedness to any other player is −1/(N−1) (Patel et al. 2020). Letting players meet themselves removes the effect: a lone tit-for-tat then also plays its own kind (itself, worth 30 points a game), and Flip 1 disappears.

Flip 2, large populations: too rare to get going

Tit-for-tat earns more than always-defect only once it makes up more than a share 1/(22−T) of the population, about 1 in 17 at T = 5. Nowak et al. call this share the invasion barrier. A single new copy must drift up to it against selection. The larger the population, the more copies that takes, and the less likely drift gets there. In Nowak et al.'s words: “In a very large population it is extremely unlikely to reach the invasion barrier when starting with a single TFT player.” Making round 1 cost tit-for-tat nothing removes its loss while rare, and Flip 2 disappears. Across the standard Prisoner's Dilemma range of T (3 to 6), Flip 2 moves from about N = 2,000 down to 1,600, while Flip 1 stays at N = 3 to 4. Above T = 6 the two flips move toward each other, and from T ≈ 17.1 tit-for-tat is not favored at any size.

In a population of groups

Suppose the population is split into groups of n that compete internally and rarely exchange migrants. A new tit-for-tat first takes over its own group or dies out there, which is Nowak's question at size n. Tit-for-tat groups then spread into defector groups through migrants. The numbers that follow are for Nowak’s T = 5. In groups of 2, tit-for-tat takes over its group less often than a neutral mutation and is not favored at any population size. In groups of 4 to 1,732, Flip 1 stays at N = 4 and Flip 2 disappears: in a group of 5, for example, two copies of tit-for-tat already earn more than the defectors (14.25 against 12 points on average at T = 5), so chance has only one copy to add. Groups larger than 1,732 show Flip 2 themselves. Tit-for-tat is then favored again once there are enough groups (Flip 3): with groups of 2,000, two groups are enough; larger groups need more of them.
